For each of the four factors we fitted a straight line against the overdose rate across the 45 counties. Then we ran every fit again with McDowell County removed. McDowell has the highest rate in the state (180.7 deaths per 100,000), and with only 45 counties, one extreme point can drag a line a long way.

Mental distress came out on top (r = 0.51, R² = 0.26). Counties where more adults report frequent mental distress tend to have higher overdose rates, by about 10.6 deaths per 100,000 for every extra percentage point. That sounds like a lot until you look at R². It explains roughly a quarter of the differences between counties, so three quarters is still unexplained.

Median income comes second and points the other way (r = -0.44, R² = 0.19). Richer counties have fewer overdose deaths, about 1.6 fewer per 100,000 for every extra $1,000 in median income. Both of these relationships survive when McDowell is dropped, but both get weaker (0.26 to 0.21 for distress, 0.19 to 0.13 for income). So McDowell was making the pattern look stronger than it really is.

Unemployment shows almost nothing. With McDowell in, r is 0.14 (R² = 0.02). With McDowell out, r drops to -0.03 (R² = 0.001), which is flat. Mental health providers are weak too (r = 0.16, R² = 0.03, or 0.07 without McDowell), and the sign surprised us. Counties with more providers per person have slightly higher overdose rates, not lower. One possible reason is that bigger, more urban counties have both more providers and more drug use, but we did not test that, so it stays a guess.

One thing makes these results harder to read. The factors overlap with each other. Mental distress and median income are correlated at -0.78, and unemployment sits close to both (0.52 with distress, -0.53 with income). In practice the first two are probably describing the same thing, poorer counties, from two sides. A single-factor line cannot separate them. That is the reason RQ3 puts all four into one model.

The factors also cover different years (income 2019 to 2023, distress 2022, unemployment 2023, providers 2024), while the overdose rates cover 2020 to 2022. The match is close but not exact.

Conclusion

Overdose deaths are high almost everywhere in West Virginia. Of the 45 counties with data, 44 are above the national rate of 31.3, and 24 are more than twice as high. The range inside the state is huge though, from 27.4 in Randolph to 180.7 in McDowell.

Among the factors we tested, frequent mental distress and low income go with higher overdose rates most clearly, but only to a moderate degree. Unemployment and mental health provider numbers add little on their own. When we put all four factors into one model, it predicted overdose rates for unseen counties a bit better than just guessing the average, with a mean error of 30.75 against 34.46, and it won in 81 of 100 random splits. A gain of about 3.7 deaths per 100,000, with wide spread between splits, is real but small. We would not call this model accurate.

The limits are important here. These are county averages, so we cannot say anything about individual people. A county with high distress and high overdose deaths does not show that distressed people are the ones overdosing, which is the ecological fallacy. The direction could also run backwards, since a county hit hard by overdose deaths may report worse mental health because of it. There are only 45 counties, and the 10 dropped for having fewer than 10 deaths are small, rural ones, so the picture leans toward larger counties. Finally, the data stops at 2022, before overdose deaths fell nationally in 2024, so this describes the peak of the crisis and not today.

Taken together, the results point to poverty and mental health as the places to look more closely. They do not tell us what causes what. Answering that would take individual-level data and a design that can handle the overlap between these factors.
